## Working Name

The product is currently tracked as **GrowOS / GreenhouseOS** until customer discovery determines the strongest market-facing name.

- **GrowOS** fits cannabis cultivation and broader commercial crop operations.
- **GreenhouseOS** fits controlled-environment agriculture beyond cannabis.
- A future naming decision may use one as the platform name and the other as a vertical edition.

## Research-Backed Product Direction

GrowOS / GreenhouseOS is a future AI-assisted cultivation operating system for commercial controlled-environment agriculture. Cannabis is the proposed first proving ground because it combines high-value crops, cultivar variation, strict operational workflows, expensive mistakes, environmental sensitivity, and regulated plant tracking.

The long-term platform may expand into commercial flowers, propagation, nurseries, fruits, vegetables, herbs, and other greenhouse crops through crop-specific templates and integrations.

## Core Positioning

> **GrowOS is the cultivation workflow and intelligence layer for commercial growers. It connects compliance records, facility SOPs, sensor history, observations, and team execution so operators can detect drift earlier, enforce playbooks more consistently, and learn what improved the next harvest.**

The enterprise cost-elimination research adds a second, financially focused positioning:

> **Keep the hardware that already works. Keep METRC for compliance. Replace the fragmented software and manual operating layer around them.**

GrowOS should be designed as a cultivation operating system that lowers the cost of running commercial cultivation through software consolidation, labor reduction, faster workflows, better exception handling, and preservation of existing hardware investments.

GrowOS should not initially become:

- another generic seed-to-sale ERP,
- a METRC replacement,
- a proprietary controller or sensor company,
- an AI plant doctor based on one image,
- or an autonomous system that runs the grow.

### Product relationship

- **METRC or another track-and-trace platform:** regulated compliance identity and reporting
- **Existing probes and controllers:** environmental, irrigation, and equipment data
- **GrowOS:** facility playbooks, task execution, observations, anomaly visibility, human decisions, reporting consolidation, and outcome learning

## Core Product Rule

**The company defines the cultivation method. AI watches execution, combines evidence, identifies risk, suggests actions, and records outcomes. Humans retain control over material cultivation, compliance, treatment, and automation decisions.**

## Core Data Principle

The platform should combine multiple sources of truth:

- **Compliance identity:** METRC or another jurisdictional track-and-trace system
- **Environmental truth:** climate, substrate, irrigation, reservoir, and equipment sensors
- **Visual truth:** scheduled camera stills and human-uploaded images
- **Operational truth:** SOPs, tasks, feeding records, treatments, and employee execution
- **Human truth:** cultivator observations, notes, approvals, and corrective actions
- **Outcome truth:** yield, grade, quality, labor, resource use, incidents, and crop loss
- **Financial truth:** subscriptions, labor hours, downtime, consulting, implementation cost, and measured savings

The AI reasoning layer should never pretend one signal is sufficient when the evidence is incomplete.

## Proposed Initial Wedge

The recommended first product is not full autonomous cultivation control. It is:

> **Facility-specific SOP execution and anomaly visibility across rooms, batches, cultivars, stages, and teams.**

The cost-elimination version of that wedge is:

> **Replace generic task, SOP, reporting, room-reset, issue, photo, and manager follow-up systems with one cultivation-native operating workflow.**

The first version should combine:

- facility, room, zone, batch, cultivar, and stage records,
- company-defined SOPs and cultivation playbooks,
- task generation by day, week, stage, room, or cultivar,
- employee assignment and completion evidence,
- structured observations and photo uploads,
- missed-task and exception workflows,
- room-reset and sanitation workflows,
- manager review,
- daily and weekly reporting,
- limited environmental context,
- METRC read context and reconciliation support,
- and AI summaries of what changed and what needs inspection.

Initial value should come from daily workflow adoption, software consolidation, labor savings, consistency, exception handling, traceability, reporting reduction, and earlier human inspection. Controller execution should come later and require explicit approval, safeguards, calibration checks, and complete audit logs.

## First 90-Day Cost Targets

GrowOS V1 should be evaluated by which costs disappear or shrink within the first 90 days.

Initial targets:

- separate task and checklist software,
- paper and spreadsheet SOP systems,
- room-reset and sanitation logs,
- photo folders and informal issue tracking,
- manager follow-up spreadsheets,
- hand-built daily and weekly reports,
- and portions of internal METRC reconciliation labor.

Yield improvement and crop-loss reduction should be treated as upside, not guaranteed baseline ROI.

The modeled $20K-$60K annual savings range for a mid-size facility may equal one or more employee salaries, but GrowOS should not be positioned primarily as a headcount-reduction system. The stronger message is that it removes avoidable administrative work and redirects skilled people toward plant care, production, oversight, and improvement.

## AI and Monitoring Model

GrowOS should use one shared AI platform with tenant-isolated operational data, private memory, SOPs, rules, room baselines, cultivar history, and optional enterprise customization.

Each customer does not need a separate fully trained large model.

For cannabis monitoring:

- hourly still capture may be normal,
- lightweight screening may run frequently,
- deeper reasoning should be scheduled or triggered,
- material recommendations require human review,
- and AI usage should be tracked and billed through included credits plus additional purchases.

Routine monitoring should be estimated during quoting using facility count, room count, camera count, capture cadence, screening cadence, reasoning cadence, storage retention, and expected anomaly volume.

## Data Ownership Direction

Recommended default:

- customers own raw operational data,
- customer-specific learned intelligence stays private,
- no cross-customer training by default,
- SLAI owns the platform and generalized algorithms,
- and shared research participation requires explicit opt-in and clear contractual boundaries.

Trust statement:

> **GrowOS learns your operation for you. It does not teach your competitors how you grow.**

## Hardware and Partner Ecosystem

GrowOS should preserve usable customer hardware and initially work through integrations, referrals, reselling, implementation, and support rather than manufacturing equipment.

Potential compatibility labels:

- **GrowOS Compatible** — supported data exchange under documented conditions
- **GrowOS Verified** — tested against defined functional, reliability, and security requirements
- **GrowOS Preferred** — verified, commercially partnered, and fully supported

Commercial partnership must remain separate from technical compatibility.

## First Customer Hypothesis

The strongest first customer is a mid-size indoor or greenhouse cannabis operator with:

- multiple rooms,
- repeated stage-based SOPs,
- meaningful staff coordination,
- high crop-value exposure,
- fragmented workflow and reporting tools,
- and enough operational pain to justify low-five-figure annual software spending.

The likely first economic buyer is a Director of Cultivation, cultivation manager, owner, COO, or operations leader. The CFO case should be based on measured savings, not feature count.

## Pilot Hypothesis

A credible first pilot should be limited to one facility, selected rooms, a defined production-cycle window, existing SOPs converted into playbooks, team task execution, observations, room-reset workflows, manager exceptions, reporting consolidation, and limited read-only environmental data.

The pilot should measure:

- daily active use,
- subscriptions removed or reduced,
- task completion visibility,
- missed-task rate,
- unresolved-exception age,
- time from abnormality to inspection,
- manager time spent chasing execution,
- reporting hours saved,
- room-reset turnaround,
- duplicate recordkeeping,
- METRC reconciliation time,
- operator confidence in summaries,
- and willingness to pay for continued use.
